## city of gold

> The country hath more quantity of gold, by manifold, than the best parts of the Indies, or Peru
>
> — Sir Walter Raleigh, on Guiana and the city called El Dorado (The Discovery of Guiana, 1596)

Releigh returned to England with tales of a place called El Dorado. A city of gold. The gold was so abundant that the king bathed in it daily; his servants blowing gold onto his oiled skin each morning (lowk freaky). Raleigh's claims authored centuries of obsession.

Dozens of expeditions disappeared into the Amazon, consumed by a wilderness that cared little for ambition. Many died chasing a dream that only existed between *desire* and *delusion*.

It was never found. The [Musica](https://colombiaone.com/2024/12/28/muiscas-colombia/) people of Colombia covered their chief in gold dust during ceremonies. The ritual was real. But somewhere across oceans and languages, a ceremony became a kingdom, a kingdom became a city, and a city became a dream.

We're all chasing El Dorado: the perfect career, the ideal relationship, the life that finally feels complete. We imagine it waiting somewhere beyond the horizon, and tell ourselves the present dissatisfaction is simply the distance between us and paradise.

El Dorado is not real.

## paradise is not arrival

I used to know how to crochet. At the end, you have to tie off the final loop. Miss it, and a single pull can undo hours of work, reducing something intricate to a pile of string. It is frustrating, but at least the failure is legible. You know what came loose. You know where to begin again.

Crochet has a final loop. A life does not.

Where does *paradise* lie? We treat it as a destination, something waiting to be handed to us once we have worked, suffered, or improved enough. But who decides when we have arrived? Who is supposed to hand it over?

We often recognize what was good only after it is gone.

Complacency feels like death, so we flee it, often in circles. The relationship that once felt electric becomes suffocating, so we leave and find someone new who makes us feel alive again, until they do not. The job that promised meaning becomes routine, so we pivot. The city starts to feel dull, so we move.

“It's just not what it used to be,” we say, mistaking the fading of novelty for the failure of the thing itself.

Change is good. But movement without direction is just an elaborate standstill.

This restlessness is not accidental. We're a species for whom wanting more often meant surviving longer. The urge to seek, compare, improve, and move helped carry us here. It also made arrival almost impossible.

Jealousy is another expression of the same restlessness. Why does someone have something I don't? I deserve it more, obviously, because I'm me, and I'm better than they are... Right?

Paradise, I think, is not arrival but coherence: the alignment of values, actions, and identity into a life that holds together.

It is when the “why” behind your actions needs no witness. When growth in one part of your life no longer requires decay in another. When wealth does not corrode intimacy, mastery does not hollow out joy, and knowledge does not dissolve meaning.

When there's one version of you, and that version is the only one left to be.

## paradise is not escape

> "There is no paradise for you to escape to."
>
> — Berserk

It is one of my favorite lines from any manga. In context, it is brutal: a man who has suffered more than most characters in fiction is told that the relief he seeks will never come. There is no distant place where the suffering will end.

Maybe that is why escape is so seductive. It asks nothing of us except the departure. A different job. A different city. A different relationship. Another country, another experience, another stamp in the passport. Somewhere else, we imagine, life will finally become weightless.

There is a vision of the good life increasingly built around this idea: earn enough to remain unburdened, travel often, avoid obligations, preserve your freedom, and collect as many pleasant experiences as possible before time runs out.

I understand the appeal. I am not convinced it is enough.

Pleasure ends with the person experiencing it. The meal is eaten. The country is visited. The photograph is taken. The memory fades. Then another experience is needed to replace it.

A life centered entirely on the self must constantly feed the self.

There are many answers to this: children, ideas, art, institutions, discoveries, communities, and people whose lives are different because yours touched them. Each carries something beyond the boundaries of one body and one lifetime.

Without that continuation, what remains when the experiences are over?

What did your freedom make possible? Whom did your comfort protect? What did you create that another person can inherit? Which lives contain evidence that you were here?

There is no paradise for us to escape to.

So what are we supposed to build here?
